## Epigenomically primed lineage infidelity in early therapy resistance in B-ALL

Therapy resistance is a primary cause of cancer death. Resistance has typically been attributed to selection of genetic subclones, but increasing evidence points to changing cell identity as a mechanism of survival. Lineage plasticity has largely been documented under targeted or antigen-directed pressure, leaving open whether it is a general response to therapeutic stress and whether cells that undergo it are induced by treatment or pre-determined before it. Here we address both questions in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, where we can observe changes in cell identity following frontline cytotoxic therapy. We generated a single-cell multiomic atlas of minimal residual disease by profiling paired diagnostic and post-induction samples from 41 pediatric patients spanning major high-risk B-ALL subtypes. Integrating single-cell transcriptomic, chromatin accessibility, and targeted DNA sequencing data, we show that early chemotherapy resistance is not primarily driven by recurrent mutations. Instead, induction therapy is associated with increased heterogeneity of developmental arrest states and an increase in stem cell- and myeloid-like leukemic blasts across subtypes. Reference mapping and mutation analysis confirm that these primitive and myeloid-like cells are malignant, display distinct transcriptional and epigenetic programs, and frequently expand after treatment. Trajectory-based clustering uncovers subtype-independent patterns of arrest-state remodeling linked to signatures predictive of clinical outcome. Two independent cohorts validate these populations: CITE-Seq of paired samples confirms at the protein level that myeloid- and stem-like blasts expand after induction while losing the B-lineage antigens upon which post-induction flow depends, and multiparametric flow cytometry of 223 patient marrows detects malignant myeloid populations at diagnosis and end of induction. Chromatin accessibility links these blasts to myeloid-primed blast populations present at diagnosis, implicating epigenetic priming in therapy resistance. These findings establish epigenetically primed lineage infidelity as a mechanism of resistance to conventional cytotoxic therapy, with a pre-existing, chromatin-encoded landscape that is detectable at diagnosis and potentially targetable.
