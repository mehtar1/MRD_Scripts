## Chemotherapy selects for epigenetically primed, non-B-lineage blasts in B-ALL minimal residual disease

Minimal residual disease (MRD) following induction therapy remains a major predictor of relapse in high-risk B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), yet the mechanisms underlying early treatment resistance are poorly defined. Here, we generated a single-cell multiomic atlas of MRD by profiling paired diagnostic and post-induction samples from 41 pediatric patients spanning major high-risk B-ALL subtypes. Integrating single-cell transcriptomic, chromatin accessibility, and targeted DNA sequencing data, we show that early chemotherapy resistance is not primarily driven by recurrent mutations. Instead, induction therapy is associated with increased heterogeneity of developmental arrest states and the emergence of stem cell- and myeloid-like leukemic blasts across subtypes. Reference mapping and mutation analysis confirm that these primitive and myeloid-like cells are malignant, display distinct transcriptional and epigenetic programs, and frequently expand after treatment. Trajectory-based clustering uncovers subtype-independent patterns of arrest-state remodeling linked to signatures predictive of clinical outcome. Two independent cohorts validate these populations: CITE-Seq of paired samples confirms at the protein level that myeloid- and HSPC-like blasts expand after induction while losing the B-lineage antigens on which clinical MRD flow depends, and multiparametric flow cytometry of 223 patient marrows detects malignant myeloid populations at diagnosis and end of induction. Chromatin accessibility traces these blasts to myeloid-primed blast populations present at diagnosis, implicating lineage plasticity and epigenetic priming in therapy resistance. Together, early resistance in high-risk B-ALL reflects non-genetic plasticity and developmental reprogramming, highlighting myeloid-like blast states as an underappreciated component of MRD biology that may compromise B-cell-directed immunotherapy while offering an alternative therapeutic target.
